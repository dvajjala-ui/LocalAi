## Assistant — research response

**You don’t need a powerful local computer. We can install the software on Oracle and let you upload videos, give instructions, preview edits, and download the result through your browser.** My earlier mention of local hardware was an alternative, not a requirement.

After checking official model documentation, developer blogs, GitHub projects, and Reddit discussions, **I recommend a combination of tools for your personal editor. Oracle Free can support a limited version; professional lip-sync generation is the hardest part.**

Oracle currently documents an Always Free ARM allowance of **2 CPU cores and 12 GB RAM, without a GPU**. I still haven’t verified what your particular account has. [Oracle documentation](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/Content/FreeTier/freetier_topic-Always_Free_Resources.htm)

**This is the setup I would start with for your budget:**

| What you want | My recommended tool/model | Assessment for Oracle Free |
|---|---|---|
| Understand your instructions, describe images, identify useful shots | **Qwen3.5-2B**, compressed to 4-bit; compare with **4B** if performance allows | Candidate for short clips and sampled frames; needs benchmarking |
| Understand speech and generate captions | **Whisper through whisper.cpp**, starting with Base or Small | Supports CPU inference; process jobs sequentially |
| Align camera footage with a separate microphone recording | **Audio waveform matching**, followed by FFmpeg | A practical CPU task when both recordings contain matching sound |
| Match images/clips to narration or music | **Speech timestamps + visual analysis + an editing timeline** | Feasible for drafts; shot choice and pacing need review |
| Cut, join, crop, add images/captions, mix music and export | **FFmpeg** | Practical, with longer waits for complex renders |
| Generate a new narration voice, when needed | **Kokoro-82M** | Lightweight candidate; test your language and preferred voice |
| Change the person’s mouth movements to new speech | **Wav2Lip for experiments**; evaluate **MuseTalk/LatentSync** with GPU access | The main limitation on Oracle Free |

The model capabilities are documented in the [Qwen model cards](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-2B), [whisper.cpp project](https://github.com/ggml-org/whisper.cpp), [FFmpeg documentation](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html), and [Kokoro model card](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M). The Oracle suitability above is my engineering assessment, **not a benchmark of your server**.

There are three different workflows behind your “all three” requirement:

1. **Camera + microphone synchronization:** find the same sound in both recordings and align them. This works best when the camera recorded reference audio. Long recordings also need checks for drift. Existing editors such as Kdenlive use this matching approach. [Kdenlive audio alignment](https://docs.kdenlive.org/en/cutting_and_assembling/right_click_menu.html)

2. **Clips/images matched to a voiceover:** transcribe the narration, identify what each shot contains, and place suitable visuals at the relevant words. This is where Qwen helps. Accurate word timing can use forced alignment, such as WhisperX, although overlapping speech and some words remain difficult. [WhisperX documentation](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX)

3. **Changing lip movements:** generate new mouth imagery for the supplied speech. Wav2Lip’s implementation includes CPU inference, so a short experiment is possible. However, its original release is restricted to personal/research/noncommercial use, and I would not promise professional results or acceptable speed on your Oracle ARM server. [CPU implementation](https://github.com/Rudrabha/Wav2Lip/blob/master/inference.py), [project terms](https://github.com/Rudrabha/Wav2Lip)

For newer lip-sync options, MuseTalk reports testing an 8-second clip in approximately five minutes on a **4 GB NVIDIA GPU**. LatentSync lists **8 GB VRAM for version 1.5 and 18 GB for 1.6**. These illustrate why free software still needs substantial computing hardware. [MuseTalk](https://github.com/TMElyralab/MuseTalk), [LatentSync](https://github.com/bytedance/LatentSync)

I also checked projects closer to an existing “AI editor”:

- **[video-use](https://github.com/browser-use/video-use):** promising for instruction-driven editing. Its documented setup uses an AI coding agent and ElevenLabs transcription, so it does **not establish a completely free Oracle-only solution**.
- **[The Gargantuas Video Editor](https://github.com/TheGargantuas/The-Gargantuas-Video-Editor):** advertises a browser interface with subtitles, upscaling, and several lip-sync models. Worth investigating as a prototype, but I haven’t verified its Oracle ARM compatibility or output quality.

The [Reddit discussions](https://www.reddit.com/r/ClaudeAI/comments/1u357ff/i_use_claude_code_open_source_tools_to_edit_my/) show people combining transcription, agents, and editing tools, alongside concerns about costs and creative control. Those are useful user reports, not proof of professional quality.

**For you, I would build the first version around Qwen3.5-2B + whisper.cpp + FFmpeg on Oracle, with a browser interface and editable previews.** Start by proving a 30–60-second project: supplied footage, images, and narration → synchronized draft → corrections → final export. Treat lip regeneration as a separate short-clip experiment.

Occasional free GPU notebooks could help test lip-sync, but they cannot provide a dependable, always-running free backend; Google explicitly says Colab resources and availability are not guaranteed. [Colab limits](https://research.google.com/colaboratory/faq.html)
